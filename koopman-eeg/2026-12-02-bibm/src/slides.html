What could be

Seizures are not random: they ride on brain rhythms that last weeks

In 13 of 14 people, seizures clustered on one phase of a multi day rhythm

Implanted devices in 37 people recorded for years. Cycles most often 20 to 30 days long, stable for up to 10 years. Red dots: seizures.

Baud et al., Multi-day rhythms modulate seizure risk in epilepsy, Nat Commun 2018
Why it matters first: about 50 million people live with epilepsy (WHO), and in the classic Kwan and Brodie cohort more than 30 percent are not controlled by drugs. For them the worst part is not knowing when. Now the hopeful part. Baud and colleagues had brain implants in 37 people, recording for years. Top panel: daily epileptic activity with seizures as red dots. Middle: a 26 day rhythm hidden in it, and the seizures sit on its rising phase. Seizures follow rhythms. And it has been done once: in 2013 an implanted advisory device in Melbourne (Cook et al., Lancet Neurology) warned 11 patients with high likelihood sensitivity from 65 to 100 percent. The question is whether we can see it from scalp EEG and short clips.

Three findings

What you can take home

  1. Learn a space where EEG moves linearly, and prediction beats eleven baselines on two datasets
  2. The gain comes from choosing which rhythms to listen to
  3. Before a seizure, some learned rhythms fade more slowly, yet unseen patients stay out of reach

1 The lens

Koopman, 1931: in the right coordinates, nonlinear motion becomes linear

{{KOOPTOY}}

A four page note in PNAS. Seventy years later it became a toolkit for reading complex dynamics.

Koopman, Hamiltonian systems and transformation in Hilbert space, PNAS 1931Mezic, Nonlinear Dyn 2005
Bernard Koopman, Columbia, 1931. The idea in one sentence: do not follow the state, follow measurements of the state, and the way those measurements change is linear. Left: what we record looks like a tangle. Right: in the right coordinates the tangle splits into rhythms, each turning a fixed angle per step and shrinking by a fixed factor. A turn and a shrink are one complex eigenvalue. Igor Mezic revived this around 2005 and it took off.
1 The lens

The same lens has read fluid wakes, measles epidemics and the human cortex

Schmid 2010Proctor and Eckhoff 2015Brunton et al. 2016Susuki and Mezic 2011
The practical version is dynamic mode decomposition, from fluid mechanics (Schmid 2010, Rowley et al. 2009). Left: one mode of the vortex street behind a wing. Middle: measles in pre vaccination England, one mode is the yearly epidemic wave, with its phase on the map (Proctor and Eckhoff). Right: Bing Brunton and colleagues applied it to electrodes on the human cortex, rebuilding the recordings from a few modes. The same lens identified swing modes in power grids (Susuki and Mezic 2011) and drives linear controllers for nonlinear robots (Korda and Mezic 2018). One lens, many sciences.
1 The lens

A spectrum is a fingerprint: near a tipping point, one rhythm fades ever more slowly

{{DEMO}}
Scheffer et al., Nature 2009Maturana et al., Critical slowing down as a biomarker for seizure susceptibility, Nat Commun 2020
Live moment. Left: each dot is one rhythm of a system. Angle is its frequency, distance from the center is how long it survives. On the circle it never fades. Drag the slider slowly. The orange dot creeps to the circle, and the response to a kick rings longer and longer. Ecologists and climate scientists call this critical slowing down: before a system tips, it recovers more slowly from small kicks. And in epilepsy: Maturana and colleagues watched implanted EEG for months and found autocorrelation and variance rising in the tens of minutes to hours before seizures in 9 of 14 patients. Plant it: remember this picture, we come back to it.
1 The lens

Deep Koopman lets a network find the right coordinates by itself

Encoder, one linear step, decoder

Lusch, Kutz and Brunton trained the encoder so that one matrix moves the state forward. In epilepsy, the idea has so far steered stimulation, not predicted seizures.

Lusch, Kutz and Brunton, Deep learning for universal linear embeddings of nonlinear dynamics, Nat Commun 2018Liang et al. 2023
Read the figure left to right: the network maps a state into new coordinates (phi), one matrix K moves it forward, the decoder maps it back. For real data we do not know the right coordinates, so we learn them. Liang and colleagues used an online deep Koopman model for closed loop neurostimulation in epilepsy. Nobody had used the learned spectrum itself to predict.

2 KSNet

Follow ten seconds of patient chb01 through KSNet

{{PIPE}}
Real clip: CHB-MIT chb01_03, 2396 to 2406 s, eight of 23 channels shown
Step 1: slide a 1/8 second window along the clip, each snapshot is encoded into 16 numbers per channel. Step 2: one shared matrix K, 16 by 16, pushes the state forward one step, then again and again, three steps. Step 3: decode each predicted state and compare it with what the EEG actually did. The loss says: K must predict the future, not only describe the present. Q and A detail from the released code: the classifier input is detached, so the encoder and K learn only from reconstruction and prediction, without labels. Only the band edges and the ConvNet see labels. Training on three steps instead of one adds up to 0.02 per metric. K is a full matrix, so rhythms can couple and rotate.
2 KSNet

A learnable band decides which rhythms the classifier gets to hear

{{CIRCLE}}

Each dot is a rhythm of the learned K

Angle gives its frequency, distance from the center how long it lasts. A soft band with learned edges weights the rhythms, then a small ConvNet classifies.

Shaded: an example band. These are rhythms of the learned state, not EEG frequency bands.

KSNet, Section III.C
These are the real 16 eigenvalues the model learned for patient chb01, read off our paper figure. Frequency of a rhythm equals its angle times 256 over 2 pi. The band is soft, two sigmoids, and its edges learn from the labels. TO CONFIRM with Siji: the code applies the band to the imaginary part of each eigenvalue (the paper's soft mask figure has that axis), the paper text describes it as mode frequency. Conjugate pairs get equal weight, so the representation stays real. Careful with the words: a 40 Hz rhythm of the latent state is not 40 Hz gamma in the EEG, because the encoder is nonlinear.

The Koopman space alone loses. Choosing the rhythms wins

{{ABL}}

The objective: reconstruct, predict three steps ahead, classify

L = λ1 Lrecon + λ2 Lpred + Lcls

Reconstruction keeps the information in the state. Prediction decodes φ(Xτ)Kh and compares it with Xτ+h for h = 1, 2, 3. Classification: binary cross entropy.

Mode frequency fi = |arg λi| / (2πΔt). Decay rate Re μi with μi = log λi / Δt. Band weight σ(c(fi − flow)) σ(c(fhigh − fi)), normalized to sum to one.

Snapshots of 32 samples, latent size 16, encoder and decoder two layers of 64, Adam 0.001, λ1 = λ2 = 1.

Data and protocol

CHB-MIT: 23 patients after excluding chb24, 256 Hz, 23 channels. SIENA: 13 patients after excluding PN00, downsampled from 512 to 256 Hz, 17 channels. Unfiltered, full band to 128 Hz.

Pre-ictal: the 30 min before onset. Inter-ictal: at least 30 min from any seizure and at least 1 h before the next. 100 random 10 s segments per patient, 50 per class.

Segment level stratified 5 fold split, about 8 to 1 to 1. Leave one patient out: three splits per held out patient.
