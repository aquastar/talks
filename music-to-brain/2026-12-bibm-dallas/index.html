Part 1. Hearing loss: a quiet epidemic

WHO World Report on Hearing 2021Lin et al. 2011Livingston et al. 2024Lawrence et al. 2020ACHIEVE 2023NIDCD Quick Statistics
The motivation for Part 2, say the numbers slowly. Hearing loss is not a small problem. More than 1.5 billion people live with it today, and WHO projects 2.5 billion by 2050, one person in four. Click: it is tied to dementia. Lin and colleagues followed 639 adults for about 12 years: mild hearing loss came with about twice the risk of dementia, moderate three times, severe five times, the severe group was small. Click: the Lancet Commission on dementia, 2024, lists 14 risk factors we can change, together about 45 percent of dementia cases. Hearing loss alone is about 7 percent, tied for the largest with high LDL cholesterol. Click: and depression, across 35 studies and 147 thousand older adults, hearing loss came with 47 percent higher odds of depression. These are associations, not proof of cause. Click: the hopeful part, said honestly. The brain is read as a graph, too

EEG: computed by us from PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery. fMRI: Hong et al., PLoS ONE 2013, CC BY-SA 3.0....
Not our result, a look at the field. First, why two tools. A note lasts about 0.2 to 0.4 seconds. EEG records electrical activity hundreds of times a second, so it sees every note, but it cannot say well where in the brain. fMRI sees where to a few millimeters, but it takes one image every 1 to 2 seconds and the blood signal lags about 5 seconds behind, so many notes blur together. The scanner is also loud, it competes with the music, while EEG is quiet, cheap and portable. That is why music studies of single notes prefer EEG, and fMRI is used for longer stretches, like a whole song. Graphs are already how people read both. Left, EEG: one node per electrode. This picture is real data: ten people resting with eyes open, from a public PhysioNet set, 62 electrodes. An edge joins two electrodes whose alpha waves keep a steady phase lag, the strongest 4 percent of pairs. You can see the alpha network over the back of the head. Edges can also be the nearest electrodes, or learned by the network. Right, fMRI: one node per brain region. This picture is from a 2013 study of 23 teenagers at rest: the blue links are connections that were weaker in teens with internet gaming addiction. Edges are regions whose signals rise and fall together, or white matter fibers. Click: what people use them for. EEG graphs: finding seizures in hospital recordings, a graph network gets AUROC 0.875 on the Temple University seizure corpus. Reading emotion, DGCNN learns the edges and gets 90.4 percent on SEED. Also sleep staging and brain computer interfaces. fMRI graphs: a fingerprint, Finn and colleagues identified 117 of 126 people from their connectivity graph alone, and the same edges predicted fluid intelligence. Disease: across 26 brain disorders, damage falls more often on the hubs of the graph, Crossley 2014. And music: when people heard their favorite song, the default mode network was most connected, Wilkins 2014. If asked: partial correlation, used for fMRI edges, is the same recipe as our delta graph.

A music generator is a language model, with a pitch graph inside

graph 3 of 7 pitch graph, learned as a helix

The graph the model learned matches the knowledge graph of music theory.

Copet et al. 2023Défossez et al. 2022Yagi et al. 2026
For a CS audience, the one idea: a music generator like MusicGen is a language model. EnCodec, a neural audio codec, turns every 20 milliseconds of sound into 4 integers from a vocabulary of 2048, so a song becomes a sequence of tokens, like words. A transformer, the same kind as GPT, predicts the next token, and the codec turns tokens back into sound. Click: music theory has a graph for pitch. The notes C D E F G A B in a row, and every note linked to the same note one octave up, the orange edges for C. That is a small knowledge graph. Click: what the model learned. Yagi and colleagues feed single notes into music models and look at the internal states, the activation vectors, projected to three dimensions: they form a helix, one turn per octave, so C3, C4 and C5 line up. That is the orange edge of the pitch graph, found inside the model, which was never told about octaves. The learned graph and the knowledge graph agree. Do music generation models encode music theory?

graph 3 of 7 chord graph, learned distances, again

Learned from chord sequences alone, the chord space follows the geometry of music theory.

Ebrahimzadeh, Bernardes, Stober, ISMIR 2026, Fig. 1a, Table 2 | Cohn 2012 (Tonnetz)
Left, music theory as a graph, the Tonnetz, which goes back to Euler in 1739. Notes are nodes. Across is a fifth, plus 7 half steps, C to G. Up right is a major third, plus 4, C to E. Then every small triangle is a chord. Point up is major: root, plus 4, plus 7, C E G. Point down is minor: root, plus 3, plus 7. Two triangles that share an edge share two notes, so you move between them by changing one note. The animation runs by itself: C major has exactly three such neighbors, P to C minor, R to A minor, L to E minor. These are the neo-Riemannian moves, after Hugo Riemann the music theorist, not the mathematician. I read this lattice as a knowledge graph of music: concepts and their relations. Click: does a model learn these distances on its own? Ebrahimzadeh, Bernardes and Stober, ISMIR 2026, train word2vec, the same skip-gram model as for words, on chord sequences from the Beethoven piano sonatas and the Beatles, with every song also transposed to all 12 keys. Each of the 24 chords gets a vector. Their Figure 1a is the map of those vectors. Two links between the two pictures. First, the fifths are the shared backbone: the animation walks D, A, E, B across the lattice, one fifth per step, and the orange halo walks the same chords one step at a time around the ring. Click: second, a key is one connected region on both sides. The six chords of C major, D minor, F, A minor, C, E minor, G, form one strip of triangles on the lattice and one arc of the ring in the model. One difference worth saying if asked: on the lattice, neighbors share two notes. In the model, neighbors are used together, for example A minor and D are the two and the five of G major. Click: the key statement. The chords fall on a ring in circle of fifths order, C, G, D, A and so on, which is the across row of the lattice closed into a ring. Numbers from their Table 2, Beethoven corpus: zero errors in the order around the ring, and for every chord its two nearest neighbors are its two fifths. Watch the orange halo on the ring: it follows the chord of the animation on the left. C to G is one step on the ring, a fifth. But the P, R and L neighbors, C minor, A minor, E minor, sit far from C on the ring: this model learned which chords appear in the same key, the fifths, not which chords share two notes. One axis of the lattice, not all of it. Be clear about the limit: this is a small model trained on chord symbols, not a generator trained on raw sound. Whether MusicGen, which learns from sound, has the same chord distances is, as far as I know, untested. That is the open question. Background if asked: Wei and colleagues, ISMIR 2024, showed that chord, interval and key labels can be read out of one layer of MusicGen and Jukebox with a tiny classifier, 93 to 98 percent, but they did not test the distances. First, a probe reads music theory (Wei et al., their Figure 1 shows the setup: synthetic clips labeled with tempo, interval, chord and so on, a frozen codec and decoder, and a small probe on one layer). Second, a July 2026 preprint feeds 36 single notes, C3 to B5, to Jukebox and MusicGen large, projects each layer to 3D and fits a helix: both form one, clearer in deeper layers, Jukebox mean helicality 2.28 and MusicGen 1.16 against 0.37 for random points, and the shape depends on the instrument, a Hammond organ winds once per octave, a clean electric guitar twice. Third, Kanatas et al. at ISMIR 2026 study 12 music models including MusicGen small, medium and large: a key probe projected on the circle of fifths stays consistent under transposition, and tonal tasks peak in the deepest layers. Our reading, not theirs: a helix is the semitone chain wound by the octave, the circle of fifths is a 12 node cycle, so these are graphs too, but graphs of where a single state sits. Neither paper reads how one state follows the next, and that transition graph is what our plan builds. Then bridge to steering: and to change the music, people still push a vector. Details of the probe study: Wei et al. built SynTheory, synthetic clips where one concept changes at a time: tempo, time signature, notes, intervals, scales, chords, chord progressions. They froze the generators and trained tiny classifiers on each layer. Concepts are linearly readable. Plain chroma features tie the models on static notes and lose on progressions. Castellon et al. found Jukebox layers beat tag trained representations by about 30 percent, most on key detection. Bridge: a probe tells us what is stored, not how the states move. That is the graph question.
